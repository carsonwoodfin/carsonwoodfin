Featured Project

Clinical Complexity, Neighborhood Vulnerability, and Anti-Amyloid Therapy Receipt

This exploratory secondary analysis examined whether neighborhood social vulnerability and clinical complexity were associated with anti-amyloid therapy receipt among patients seen at the Washington University Memory Diagnostic Center.

Using R, I evaluated treatment receipt across Social Vulnerability Index quartiles, comorbidity burden, and selected medication burden. Descriptive analyses, bias-reduced logistic regression, sequential modeling, and sensitivity analyses were used to evaluate whether clinical burden helped explain previously observed neighborhood differences in treatment receipt.

The findings showed that anti-amyloid therapy receipt declined as neighborhood vulnerability increased. Higher comorbidity and selected medication burden were also associated with lower treatment receipt, but these clinical factors did not fully explain the neighborhood gradient.

Key Findings

Neighborhood Vulnerability

Anti-amyloid therapy receipt declined from 10.1% in the most advantaged SVI quartile to 1.4% in the most vulnerable quartile.

Clinical Complexity

High comorbidity burden and high selected medication burden were both associated with lower odds of treatment receipt.

Persistent Gradient

Adjustment for clinical burden reduced the neighborhood association only modestly, suggesting that clinical complexity was part of the pattern but did not fully account for it.
